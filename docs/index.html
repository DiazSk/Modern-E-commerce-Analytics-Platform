Black Friday week sent more shoppers to the cart — and a four-day tracking gap nearly told the opposite story.

Across 109.8M real events from one online store, sessions reaching the cart rose from 9.2% in the four weeks before to 11.7% during Black Friday week, and purchases from 5.25% to 5.60%. Counted naively, the same data says the opposite: on Nov 15 the store logged 468,262 carts and zero purchases.

What the gap did

Leave Nov 14–17 in the baseline and every headline flips: cart reach −1.7 pp, purchase rate −0.08 pp, abandonment −12.3 pp. Nov 15 recorded carts with no purchases; Nov 17 recorded 185,195, about eight times a normal day.

Read these before quoting a number

  1. Nov 14–17 is excluded from every baseline. The source logged 468,262 carts and zero purchases on Nov 15, then 185,195 purchases on Nov 17: a tracking failure, not shopper behavior. The explorer's toggle puts the days back so you can see what they do. A warn-level dbt test flags any day like this.
  2. “Session” is the dataset's session ID. Most last about a minute (median 61 s), but 0.28% span more than a day and the longest runs 60 days. Sessions are dated by when they started.
  3. No order IDs exist in the source, so “purchases” are purchase events, not orders.
  4. Units overlap by category: one session can count in several categories, so category-level intervals are somewhat optimistic. The headline cart and purchase rates are session-level.
  5. Price bands use each product's latest price, which for some products is a Black Friday price.
  6. These are associations, not effects. Black Friday week also changes who shops; test before acting.
  7. Method: differences in proportions with 95% Wald intervals, diff ± 1.96·√(p₁(1−p₁)/n₁ + p₀(1−p₀)/n₀); groups under 1,000 observations are left out of comparisons.
